The PIN-Browser Manifesto

Why I built a browser that remembers for you, works for you, and answers only to you.

The window and the workshop

For thirty years the web browser has been a window. A remarkably good one: fast, secure, and capable of rendering almost anything. But a window is a passive thing. You look through it, the view changes, and whatever you saw is gone the moment you look away. Today's browsers are, at heart, viewports onto other people's servers. They are optimized to show you the Internet, not to help you use it.

That gap is the reason PIN-Browser exists. I believe a browser should be less like a window and more like a workshop: a place where information comes in, gets examined, gets shaped into something useful, and is kept for the day you need it again. And I believe the person who owns that workshop should be the only one who holds the keys.

We forget what we have seen

The research on how people actually use the web is remarkably consistent. We return to the same pages again and again; Tauscher and Greenberg found that 58% of page visits were revisits[13], and later studies confirmed that revisiting is a central, enduring web behavior[14][23][26]. Yet the tools for getting back are weak. History lists are hard to scan[16], people re-search for things they have already found[24][31], and open tabs become an anxious, improvised filing system that users neither trust nor want to close[36]. Meanwhile the web itself decays: Pew Research found that 38% of webpages that existed in 2013 were no longer accessible a decade later[33].

The idea of a personal, searchable record of everything you have read is not new. Vannevar Bush imagined the Memex in 1945[1]; Microsoft Research's MyLifeBits pursued it in earnest[6]; Dumais and colleagues showed the value of simply being able to search "stuff I've seen"[45]. What was missing was a browser that made this ordinary, automatic, and private.

What PIN-Browser does today

PIN-Browser is a day-log browser for Windows and Mac. While the day log is on, it keeps a quiet record of your browsing in a folder you choose, one folder per day: periodic screenshots of the window, the page address, title and visible text, copies of pages as they looked, the recordings you make, and the files you download. From that record it builds things a window never could:

  • Retrieval of everything you have seen. Dial back (Ctrl+Shift+D) lets you step back through your day visually and search page text across days, so "that chart I saw on Tuesday" is findable even if you never bookmarked it.
  • Deeper analysis. The Browsing Report turns raw browsing into an honest picture of where your time and attention went, a mirror that self-reports routinely get wrong[93].
  • Work saved for future review. Pages, recordings and downloads are kept together with the moment you found them, so research survives closed tabs, changed pages and dead links.
  • Discretion built in. A local adult-content check can blur sensitive pages, so reviewing your history never has to be awkward.

Your data does not leave your computer

A browser that remembers everything carries a serious obligation. Most Americans already feel they have little control over the data companies collect about them[115][120], and research has shown how revealing browsing histories are: they can identify a person[110] and expose traits people never chose to disclose[102]. Whole industries are built on harvesting that behavior[106].

So PIN-Browser makes a simple promise: the user's data does not leave their computer as a result of any action taken by the browser. No account, no analytics, no cloud copy. Search, reporting and the adult-content check all run locally. This follows the spirit of local-first software: you own your data, in spite of the cloud[117]. Any AI capability will be opt-in, and before anything is used you will see a preview of exactly what would be shared.

Where PIN-Browser is going

Thoughts, not just pages. Reading is an active act. People underline, annotate and argue in the margins, and those marks are often worth more than the text itself[73][74]. PIN-Browser will let you comment on, annotate and give feedback on anything you have seen, keep those thoughts attached to the exact moment and page, and, when you choose, share them with a colleague, a team or the public[76].

Analysis that matches how people really research. Real research is foraging: following scent, gathering fragments and assembling meaning over many sessions[64][54]. A browser that holds your whole trail can help you see the shape of what you have gathered instead of leaving it scattered across tabs and days[62][37].

An agentic future that starts from your history. Web agents are improving quickly[135][137][141], but today they arrive knowing nothing about you, or they ask you to hand your life to someone else's server. PIN-Browser takes the opposite approach: with your consent, the agent of your choice can work from your own browsing history, on your own machine. That makes possible things no generic assistant can do:

  • Recall on demand. "Find the article about battery recycling I read last week" answered from what you actually saw, with the screenshot to prove it.
  • Research briefs grounded in your reading. A summary of everything you read on a topic this month, with a citation back to every page and the moment you saw it.
  • Comparison from pages you already viewed. Laptops, apartments or flights you browsed across three sites, laid side by side without starting over.
  • Picking up where you left off. Interrupted tasks are the norm, not the exception[79]; an agent can reconstruct an abandoned task and finish the tedious steps[32].
  • A job search that keeps itself organized. Every posting you opened, when you applied, and which companies you researched, turned into a living tracker.
  • Insight into your own attention. Gentle, private reflection on how your browsing serves your goals, in the tradition of digital self-control research[91].
  • Faster browsing. An agent that has watched how you work can fill the repetitive forms and run the routine checks you do every day, and show its work before acting.

The principles are non-negotiable: the user stays in charge[123][125], agents ask before they act, and untrusted web content is never allowed to steer an agent toward your data[134].

Why I am building it

I am a product manager who has spent a career turning ambitious ideas into software people can actually use. PIN-Browser is that conviction made concrete: start from real human behavior, grounded in decades of research; ship something useful now; earn trust with an uncompromising stance on privacy; and design for the agentic future without surrendering control to it. The browser has been a window long enough. It is time to give people a workshop of their own.

— Vic Parekh, November 2026
